# Source Notes and Reconciliation

## Purpose

Use this file when a style rule needs justification, when two recommendations appear to conflict, or when adapting the skill to a particular mathematical genre.

The skill synthesizes five supplied sources rather than treating any one author's preferences as universal law.

## Paul R. Halmos — *How to Write Mathematics*

Primary contributions to this skill:

- mathematical writing is fundamentally communication;
- write for a definite reader;
- organize material to minimize reader resistance;
- design notation deliberately;
- rewrite repeatedly rather than merely patching drafts;
- use good ordinary language together with precise mathematical language;
- maintain mathematical honesty about what is proved, assumed, obvious, deferred, or unknown;
- remove irrelevant assumptions and trivial distractions;
- state the theorem before the proof;
- use strategic repetition for parallel structures;
- use technical terms consistently;
- resist unnecessary symbols and labels;
- punctuate mathematical clauses as prose;
- balance prose and symbolic material visually.

Halmos often states preferences strongly. Treat the underlying reader-effort principle as more important than literal obedience to every local stylistic preference.

## Donald E. Knuth, Tracy Larrabee, and Paul M. Roberts — *Mathematical Writing*

Primary contributions:

- put words between adjacent formulas;
- do not start sentences with symbols;
- prefer words to formal logic symbols in ordinary exposition;
- make theorem statements locally self-contained;
- think of author and reader as a dialogue;
- write with sentence rhythm and left-to-right readability;
- use parallelism for parallel ideas;
- provide running commentary rather than homework-style formula sequences;
- define variables and explain important ideas in complementary ways;
- motivate what follows;
- assume many readers skim formulas initially;
- keep notation consistent and minimize subscripts;
- number only useful displays;
- give special attention to openings;
- avoid unnecessary jargon and noun stacks;
- revise in multiple passes;
- use diagrams when they explain complex structure more efficiently than prose.

Knuth's course material also demonstrates the value of before/after editing and of comments that keep the reader synchronized with goals and subgoals.

## Steven Kleiman — *Writing a Math Phase Two Paper*

Primary contributions to the synthesis:

- treat a mathematical paper as a designed expository object rather than a transcript of work performed;
- make the beginning and abstract useful to a busy reader;
- structure the paper so definitions, statements, proofs, and transitions serve the reader;
- use mathematical prose and notation carefully and consistently;
- make the paper readable as an independent piece of mathematical exposition.

The supplied PDF has a damaged text layer, so its content should be checked from rendered pages when exact wording matters. Do not rely on corrupted extracted text.

## John N. Tsitsiklis — *A Few Tips on Writing Papers with Mathematical Content*

Primary contributions:

- identify audience, main point, table of contents, results, notation, and terminology before drafting;
- use manageable sections with explicit guidance;
- make the abstract crisp and the introduction problem-first;
- compare related work rather than merely list it;
- keep the main text self-contained even when proofs move to appendices;
- prefer active voice and parallel constructions;
- write proofs forward and close to linear;
- keep theorem statements crisp;
- summarize proof flow when useful;
- use examples, counterexamples, and figures;
- minimize reader effort through reminders;
- audit ambiguous quantifiers and hidden constant dependence;
- avoid unsupported uniqueness claims;
- treat mathematical sentences as ordinary grammatical sentences;
- pay attention to LaTeX spacing and mathematical typography.

Tsitsiklis is especially useful for modern research-paper structure and applied mathematics/operations research.

## Dimitri Bertsekas — *Ten Simple Rules for Mathematical Writing*

Primary contributions:

- distinguish broad goals from verifiable composition rules;
- organize writing into reader-sized segments;
- order arguments linearly and keep prerequisites close to use;
- use hierarchical development when material is genuinely reused;
- maintain consistent notation and nomenclature;
- state related results in parallel form;
- neither overexplain nor underexplain relative to the target audience;
- tell readers where the exposition is going;
- use suggestive references instead of naked equation numbers;
- use examples, counterexamples, and visualization deliberately;
- maintain precision even in intuitive or proof-light mathematical writing.

Bertsekas's composition rules are particularly suitable for agentic use because many can be checked mechanically or procedurally.

## Reconciliation rules

When the sources differ, do not manufacture a false consensus. Apply this order:

1. correctness;
2. unambiguous mathematical meaning;
3. reader-oriented logical structure;
4. internal consistency;
5. venue or journal style;
6. author preference.

Examples of legitimate variation:

- pronouns: neutral declarative prose versus editorial “we”;
- equation numbering: sparse numbering versus venue-wide numbering conventions;
- passive voice: generally avoid, but use selectively when agency is irrelevant;
- theorem self-containment: maximize local readability, but do not repeat a large standing context mechanically in every statement.

The skill should behave as a disciplined editor, not as a rigid style checker.
